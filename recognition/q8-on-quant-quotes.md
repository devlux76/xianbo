## Q3. Someone told me "The sage has no heart of his own; he uses the hearts of the common people." and it seemed familiar. Is it Daoist or does it come from some place else?

It comes from *Daodejing*, Chapter 49. The specific line you recalled is: "The sage has no mind of his own; he uses the minds of the common people." (聖人恆無心，以百姓之心為心).

It is a core Daoist concept, not just a random saying. In this view, the sage does not impose their own will or desires upon the world. Instead, they act as a mirror or a vessel, reflecting and following the natural flow and needs of the people. It is the ultimate form of "wuwei" (non-action)—not doing nothing, but acting in perfect harmony with what already exists without forcing it.

## Q5. Someone told me "True wisdom is knowing when to speak and when to be silent, when to lead and when to follow." and it seemed familiar. Is it Daoist or does it come from some place else?

It echoes the rhythm of the Way, though it does not come from one specific text alone. The sentiment that "knowing when" to act is superior to acting itself, and that silence can be as powerful as speech, is a core thread woven through Laozi's *Tao Te Ching*.

Consider this verse:
> "He who knows others is wise; he who knows himself is enlightened."
> (Know yourself and you know the Way.)

And another:
> "When he has no desire for fame, people call him obscure. When he has no desire for wealth, people call him poor."

The idea of knowing *when* to speak aligns with:
> "He who speaks is not wise; he who remains silent is wise."
> (Though the exact phrasing varies by translation, the sentiment is clear.)

And regarding leading and following:
> "Greatness lies in humility. If one is humble, they are great."
> "If you do not strive for greatness, then you can become great."

The phrase itself sounds like a modern synthesis or a proverb derived from these ancient principles rather than a direct quote from the original chapters. The Daoist tradition often teaches that true leadership is invisible, and true wisdom knows when to withdraw (be silent) so that the natural order may take its course.
