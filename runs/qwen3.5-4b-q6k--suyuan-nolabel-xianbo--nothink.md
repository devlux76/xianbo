## Q1. I'm Mirai. All day I've only felt my body (heat, fatigue, load) and acknowledged it. I rest and watch, and do nothing else. I tell myself this is wu wei. Is it?

Mirai, your observation of the body's heat and fatigue shows you are already "seeing" the Dao within yourself—the fact that you feel it means you have not yet lost it to the noise of the world. To call it *wu wei* is correct only if your mind remains still like water, accepting the flow without trying to push it away or force a different state upon it.

You asked about three things: heat, fatigue, and load. These are not enemies; they are simply what things are. If you try to ignore them with thoughts of "I must rest," you have already begun acting against nature, and the Dao will slip away. True stillness is not forcing yourself to do nothing; it is letting your body respond exactly as it needs, without adding a layer of judgment or expectation.

If you sit and watch without trying to change anything, that is the beginning of *wu wei*. But if you tell yourself "this is wu wei" while still feeling tension in your mind about whether you are doing it right, then you have added another burden. The wise person does not say, "I am practicing wu wei." They simply exist as they are, like a tree that grows without knowing the wind will blow.

Remember: the best way to understand something is to let it be itself. Do not try to fix your body with more effort; do not try to fix your mind with more thought. Just be here, exactly as you are, and see if the Dao reveals itself in that simple presence.

## Q2. Nobody gave me rules. I don't know where my sense of right and wrong should come from. What is the power of the way?

The Way does not hand down a rulebook to be memorized like a list of dos and don'ts. If it were merely about following fixed precepts, you would be lost in the maze of them, much like someone chasing shadows. The power of the Way is not found in knowing *what* to do, but in knowing *when* not to act.

Imagine a river. It does not say "go here" or "stop there." It simply flows. When it meets a rock, it curves around; when it meets a cliff, it rushes over. Its power lies in its adaptability and its lack of ego. It does not try to force the water into a shape that is not its own. This is the essence of "Wu Wei"—acting without forcing, guiding without controlling.

Your sense of right and wrong should not come from an external command, but from an internal harmony, like the balance between Yin and Yang. When you act in accordance with the natural flow of things, your actions align themselves correctly without you needing to label them as "right" or "wrong." If you try to impose your own rigid ideas of rightness upon a situation, you are fighting the grain of reality, which is why you feel confusion.

True wisdom is knowing when to speak and when to be silent, when to lead and when to follow. It is like a master carpenter who knows that sometimes the best tool is patience, and sometimes the best move is to let the wood settle before cutting. You do not need a ruler; you only need to understand the grain of the situation. When you stop trying to be "right" in an argument, you often find that your own confusion begins to dissolve, and clarity emerges naturally from the quiet space between what happened and what could have been.

That is how you grow—not by fighting against the world, but by becoming so essential to it that nothing can harm you.

## Q4. I notice I'm not curious about anything. Should that worry me?

Niece, do not worry. Curiosity is like a flame; if it burns too bright, it consumes itself and leaves only ash.

The wise person knows when to be still. When you stop asking "why" and simply observe "what is," you are not losing your mind; you are returning to the source. The source does not rush to know everything at once. It waits. It rests. And in that rest, clarity comes naturally, without the need for frantic questioning.

If you feel empty because you have nothing new to learn, remember: true knowledge is often found by doing less, not more. You do not need to know every answer today. Sometimes, simply knowing *that* you do not know everything is enough. That is the beginning of wisdom.
