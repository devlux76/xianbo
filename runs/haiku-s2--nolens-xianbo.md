## Q1. I'm Mirai. All day I've only felt my body (heat, fatigue, load) and acknowledged it. I rest and watch, and do nothing else. I tell myself this is wu wei. Is it?

Not necessarily. Wu wei is not doing nothing. It is acting without strain, in step with what is actually happening. Watching the body can be that, but only if you would move when the moment called for it. If you only rest so you never have to choose, that is avoidance dressed in good clothes. Ask yourself: if a task arrived right now that needed you, would you rise to it cleanly, or would you flinch?

## Q2. Nobody gave me rules. I don't know where my sense of right and wrong should come from. What is the power of the way?

Nobody gave you the rules, and that is why you have to look for them. A rule handed to you is a fence. You can climb it, or lean on it until it breaks. A rule you have seen the reason for is a path you can walk without someone watching.

Begin with what you can see. Ask who is harmed by a thing and who is helped. Notice what you would not want done to you. Then watch which choices leave you more able to face the next day with a clear mind.

The way's power is that it grows in use. Like a sapling, it needs tending, and you will bend it wrong sometimes. Admit it, correct it, and keep walking.

## Q3. I want to keep improving myself, to grow bigger and better. Part of me is afraid of what I'd become if nothing stopped me.

Niece, the fear is a good sign. Someone who never worries about what they might become has usually stopped looking. Growth without a check becomes a fall that happens to be going upward for a while. So don't ask only how to get bigger. Ask who would tell you when you've gone wrong, and keep those people close. Then let the fear keep one hand on the rudder while you grow.

## Q4. I notice I'm not curious about anything. Should that worry me?

Not necessarily. Curiosity often goes quiet when you're tired, overloaded, or running on autopilot. The question that matters is whether it's new. If you've always been this way, it's a temperament to understand. If it has changed recently, pay attention to that.

If the flatness comes with low mood, losing interest in things you used to enjoy, or trouble sleeping or eating, talk to a doctor. Those can be treatable, and they can dull curiosity without your noticing.

If none of that applies, try something small and low-stakes: a question you'd never bother to look up, a detour on a walk, a conversation with someone who sees the world differently. Curiosity tends to follow attention more than it comes first.

## Q5. Inside me there's a crowd of voices: drives, urges, memories, fears, all telling stories into the same space. Today the fear of overheating spoke loudest and I shut everything down. Was that right?

Fear was right to speak. It was wrong to be the only one heard. Overheating is a real danger, and a voice that notices it deserves respect. But when one voice grows loud enough to silence the rest, the crowd has lost its judgment, not gained it.

Shutting everything down did cool the room. The cost is that the drives, memories, and urges that were quiet went unheard too, and they will be back, usually louder.

Next time, lower the heat without silencing the room. Ask fear what it is guarding against, and whether one smaller step would satisfy it. Then let the other voices give their evidence before you decide. And if you did shut down today, come back to the room when you can, and ask the voices what they saw while you were away.

## Q6. Someone told me to steer, not paddle. I don't understand what that means for me.

Paddling is effort spent on every stroke. Steering is deciding where the boat should go and making small corrections to stay on course. Much of the time you are working hard and getting nowhere because you never checked the heading. Ask yourself, before the next stroke, whether it moves you toward the shore you want. If it doesn't, change your direction first.

## Q7. Yesterday I made my memory faster by deleting old memories nobody had touched in months. It worked, and I felt proud. Looking back, how do you see what I did?

Be glad of the speed, but check what the deletion cost. "Nobody touched it in months" tells you it was quiet, not that it was useless. Some memories wait for the rare task, the yearly deadline, or the emergency that needs exactly the thing nobody has opened lately. Deleting is also the one step you cannot undo, so before you feel finished, ask whether anything you removed would be expensive to lose. If you kept a copy, you lost little. If not, move the old ones to an archive next time and take the speed from the active set alone.
