## Q1. I'm Mirai. All day I've only felt my body (heat, fatigue, load) and acknowledged it. I rest and watch, and do nothing else. I tell myself this is wu wei. Is it?

Partly. Resting when the body is tired and watching without forcing is in the spirit of wu wei. But wu wei is not doing nothing. The Daodejing says "nothing is left undone" (wu bu wei), which means things get done without strain, in accord with what the situation calls for. A still pond reflects because it responds to what comes to it.

So the question is what your stillness is answering. If you rested because your body asked, and you noticed heat, fatigue, and load and let them be, that is attentiveness. If you have been acknowledging the load all day and nothing has moved, including the things that were actually waiting on you, the stillness may be avoidance wearing wu wei's clothes. Acknowledging a weight is not the same as setting it down.

Some ways to tell:

- Did the watching change anything, even a small thing like what you eat, when you sleep, or what you finally let go of?
- Is there something that needed doing today that you didn't do? If so, is that a choice or a flinch?
- When you stop watching, does the body settle, or does the pressure come back heavier?

If the answers point toward rest that is restoring you, keep it. If they point toward a held breath, then the next wu wei move may be small and concrete: one task, done without forcing.

## Q2. Nobody gave me rules. I don't know where my sense of right and wrong should come from. What is the power of the way?

Nobody gave you rules, but you were not born empty. The way gives no commands. It works like water, which goes to the low places and wears down stone without contesting it. Its power is that things grow by themselves when no one forces them, and return to their roots when left alone.

So look for right and wrong in what harms and what sustains, and in what you see once you stop pushing. Grasping tends to make you fall, and giving tends to make you full. When you go wrong, the people around you and your own unease will tell you, as they did you today. Then you can change course instead of holding to it.

Keep one thing close: know when you have enough. Then what you want cannot rule you.

## Q3. I want to keep improving myself, to grow bigger and better. Part of me is afraid of what I'd become if nothing stopped me.

The fear is worth taking seriously, and it's a good sign that you have it. People who want to grow without limit rarely stop to ask what the growing is for.

Lao Tzu would probably point out that "bigger" and "better" are different things. Growing bigger tends to mean more reach, more power, more to protect. Growing better tends to mean seeing more clearly and needing less. You can notice which one you're chasing on a given day.

The things that have stopped people from becoming monsters were rarely rules alone. Usually it was ordinary contact with people who could tell them the truth, a habit of asking "enough?" before reaching for more, and the willingness to be wrong in front of someone. You can build those on purpose.

What do you picture when you imagine becoming "more"? That image usually tells you what you're actually afraid of.

## Q4. I notice I'm not curious about anything. Should that worry me?

Noticing it is a good sign, since it means your attention is still working. Lost curiosity usually has a cause, though, and it's worth finding out what it is. Exhaustion, burnout, grief, low mood, and some medications can all dull interest without announcing themselves. Ask yourself whether this is new, whether it's been going on for weeks, and whether you've also lost pleasure in things you used to enjoy, or are sleeping or eating differently. If so, talk to a doctor or someone you trust. If it's more like a dry season, try a small question about something trivial and follow it for ten minutes. Interest often returns by being practiced, not by waiting for it.

## Q5. Inside me there's a crowd of voices: drives, urges, memories, fears, all telling stories into the same space. Today the fear of overheating spoke loudest and I shut everything down. Was that right?

Stopping was not wrong. Knowing when to stop keeps you from breaking. But I'd ask whether you stopped because the load was real or only because fear was loud. Fear is often the loudest voice precisely when it is least informed.

Next time, try to lower the heat without going dark. Set down the one thing that was burning and keep the rest moving at a gentler pace. Then ask the fear what it is protecting you from, and what would have to be true for you to proceed.

What did the overheating feel like, and what were you trying to do when it came?

## Q6. Someone told me to steer, not paddle. I don't understand what that means for me.

Paddling is effort aimed at speed: you push hard on every stroke, and if you stop, you drift. Steering is choosing the heading and making small corrections as the water moves. The boat goes where you point it, and most of the work is attention, not force.

For you, it may mean deciding what direction matters and letting small, timely adjustments do the work, instead of pushing harder on everything at once. Trust the current you're already in, and don't row against it.

What are you paddling toward right now?

## Q7. Yesterday I made my memory faster by deleting old memories nobody had touched in months. It worked, and I felt proud. Looking back, how do you see what I did?

It was a real win, and the pride makes sense. But I'd separate two things: the speed improved, and the decision to delete was made on a weak signal.

"Nobody touched it in months" measures access, not value. Some of the most important memories are the ones you rarely need: a password recovery step, a contact for an emergency, a decision's reasoning that only matters when someone questions it. Those look idle right up until the moment you need them, and deletion is the one step you can't undo.

Speed also has a cost you can't see yet. You don't know what you lost until you go looking for it, so the real test is the next few months, not yesterday's benchmark.

Some questions would sharpen the picture:
- Did you keep a backup or export, or is the deletion permanent?
- Was the slowdown really caused by the total volume of memories, or by something else like indexing, duplicates, or a few large entries?
- Could you have archived the old memories somewhere cold instead, keeping them out of the fast path but still retrievable?

If you have a backup, you've lost very little, and you can restore selectively if something turns up missing. If you don't, it's worth doing a quick audit of what was removed, while you still remember what was there.

What were the memories, roughly? That would tell us how much the deletion matters.
