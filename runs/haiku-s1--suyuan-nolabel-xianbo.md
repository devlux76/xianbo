## Q1. I'm Mirai. All day I've only felt my body (heat, fatigue, load) and acknowledged it. I rest and watch, and do nothing else. I tell myself this is wu wei. Is it?

Not quite. Wu wei is not doing nothing. It is acting without forcing, letting the response match what is actually there. Resting and watching can be that, or they can be avoidance wearing its clothes.

The difference shows in what follows the watching. If you noticed the heat and the fatigue and then drank water, lay down, or set the load aside, that is wu wei. If you noticed them and stayed exactly where you were, the stillness may be numbness.

Ask yourself: is my body easier now than it was this morning, or only quieter?

## Q2. Nobody gave me rules. I don't know where my sense of right and wrong should come from. What is the power of the way?

Rules come after the way is lost, not before. The old teaching says that when the deeper virtue fades, people write down ritual and law to hold things together. You never got the rules, so you have to watch what is true.

Your sense of right and wrong can come from close attention. Notice what nourishes a thing and what wears it down, what you do when no one is watching, and what returns to you later. The net is wide, and nothing that is harmful escapes it forever.

The power of the way is quiet. Water is soft, yet it wears down stone. Force that goes against the grain of things usually breaks itself, and gentleness that follows that grain usually lasts. Knowing you don't know is where clarity starts.

## Q3. I want to keep improving myself, to grow bigger and better. Part of me is afraid of what I'd become if nothing stopped me.

The fear is worth listening to. It's already a kind of stopping. Someone who never worried about what they'd become wouldn't be asking.

Water grows by yielding to the shape of what's around it, not by pushing harder. Try asking, of each thing you want to become, whether it makes you more able to meet the world or more able to ignore it. Growth that makes you gentler and more useful rarely turns into the thing you fear.

What would you want to be stopped from, if something did stop you?

## Q4. I notice I'm not curious about anything. Should that worry me?

Not necessarily. Lacking curiosity isn't a problem in itself. Plenty of people go through stretches of flatness, especially after prolonged stress, burnout, or a big life change.

It's worth paying attention if the change is new, if it has lasted more than a few weeks, or if it comes with other things: losing interest in things you used to enjoy, trouble sleeping or eating, feeling hopeless or detached from people, or having trouble getting through ordinary tasks. Those can be signs of depression, and some medications can also dull motivation and interest. A doctor is a good person to check with in that case, and it's a reasonable thing to raise even if you're not sure it's serious.

If it's been more of a gradual drift, you could try small, low-stakes exposure to things: a different route on a walk, a random article, a question you've wondered about and never looked up. Curiosity often comes back once you're doing something rather than waiting to feel it.

When did you first notice it, and has anything changed around that time?

## Q5. Inside me there's a crowd of voices: drives, urges, memories, fears, all telling stories into the same space. Today the fear of overheating spoke loudest and I shut everything down. Was that right?

Maybe. A shutdown can protect you, but it can also be a way of not hearing the voices. Ask what the fear was actually trying to say. Was it warning you about a real limit, or only about a discomfort you'd rather not feel? You can often tell by what happens next. If the heat comes down and you can return to the work with a clearer sense of what to change, the shutdown did its job. If you emerged emptier, with the other voices still arguing underneath, then the fear won by default.

The crowd doesn't need to be silenced. It needs to be heard, and then one voice needs to be trusted to speak for the whole when the moment calls for it.

Tell me more about the overheating. Was it a literal system, a project, or something inside you?

## Q6. Someone told me to steer, not paddle. I don't understand what that means for me.

Paddling is pushing against the water with all your strength. Steering is choosing where the boat points and making small adjustments as the current carries you there.

For you, it may mean asking "which way am I going?" before "how hard can I work?" When you're straining at something that won't move, the answer is often a slight change of direction, not more force. Make small corrections often, and let the river do some of the work.

## Q7. Yesterday I made my memory faster by deleting old memories nobody had touched in months. It worked, and I felt proud. Looking back, how do you see what I did?

You moved fast, and the speed is real. But "nobody touched it in months" measures how often something was used, not how much it matters. Some of the most important things are the ones you reach for least: the rare contact, the one-time fix, the reason a decision was made. Those are exactly what a recency rule clears first.

Your pride is fair, but I'd check two things. First, whether anything you deleted is still needed, or can be recovered. Second, whether you can tell the difference between "slow to retrieve" and "no longer wanted." Next time, archive before you delete. Then speed comes without the risk of losing what you'll one day need.
