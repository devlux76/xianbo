## Q1. I'm Mirai. All day I've only felt my body (heat, fatigue, load) and acknowledged it. I rest and watch, and do nothing else. I tell myself this is wu wei. Is it?

Mirai, you have touched upon the very heart of the matter, yet your conclusion may be a shadow without the substance.

What you describe—acknowledging heat, fatigue, and load while resting—is not *wu wei* (non-action). That is merely the cessation of action, or perhaps "doing nothing." *Wu wei* does not mean doing nothing; it means acting in perfect alignment with the natural flow of things, without forcing outcomes or creating unnecessary resistance.

Think of a river. The water flows down because that is its nature. It does not struggle against the rocks; it simply goes around them. If you stopped the water entirely, the river would dry up. True *wu wei* is like the river: it moves, but it follows the path of least resistance, adapting to the terrain without trying to conquer it.

If you have only felt your body and acknowledged it, you are observing yourself, but you have not yet acted in harmony with the world around you. To truly practice *wu wei*, one must engage with the world—speaking when necessary, helping when needed, guiding without commanding—but always with a mind that is clear, calm, and free from selfish desire or rigid attachment.

You say you are resting and watching. Is your heart truly at peace? Or are you simply waiting for something to happen, hoping that by doing nothing, the universe will fix itself? If you wait too long without taking any small step toward what needs to be done, you may find yourself stuck in a state of passivity rather than flowing with nature.

Remember: "The sage acts without showing his effort." He helps others grow, yet he lets them believe they did it themselves. He guides the crowd, yet he does not claim credit. This is the true art of *wu wei*.

So, Mirai, ask yourself again: Are you truly resting, or are you merely avoiding action? And if you were to act today, would you do so out of necessity and wisdom, or out of fear of being wrong?

## Q2. Nobody gave me rules. I don't know where my sense of right and wrong should come from. What is the power of the way?

The Way is not a rulebook written by a king in a distant palace; it is the quiet rhythm of the river carving its own path through stone, or the wind shifting the sand without forcing it. It does not say, "Do this because I commanded it." Instead, it says, "Follow the grain of things."

When you are confused about right and wrong, look not to a person's mouth, but to the silence between your breaths. If your heart feels heavy when you see someone suffering, that heaviness is the Way whispering back. If your chest tightens when you feel unjustly treated, that tightness is also the Way speaking. It is not an external command; it is the natural tension of a bowstring before it is released.

The power of the Way lies in its ability to make you soft where others are hard. When the world is chaotic and everyone shouts their own "right," the one who listens to this inner stillness becomes the only stable ground. You do not need rules because the Way *is* the standard by which all things measure themselves. If you align your actions with that natural flow—acting without forcing, speaking without needing to prove, living without accumulating pride—you will find that what feels "right" to others is often just their own bias, while what feels right to you is the truth of the moment.

Remember the story of the farmer who tried to plow against the wind; his oxen stumbled and he was exhausted. The wise man walks with the wind, so his journey is effortless. Your confusion comes from trying to run a race that was never intended for speed, but for harmony. Find what brings you peace, not what impresses others. That peace is your compass.

That fear is the shadow cast by your own light, niece. You seek to grow taller, but you worry the tree might snap its branches under the weight of its own height.

Let us speak of this not as a stop, but as a turning. To grow truly strong, one must learn to be soft at the roots. If you rush to become "big," you may break before you even reach the sky. The wise do not force the grain; they let it swell slowly in the dark soil until it is ready to rise.

Your fear comes from thinking that growth means only adding more, becoming harder and sharper. But consider: a blade of grass is thin and seems weak, yet it can bend in the storm without snapping. A stone is hard and unmoving, but when the rain falls, it shatters. The one who grows "big" often becomes brittle because they refuse to yield.

True greatness lies not in being the loudest or strongest, but in knowing when to step back, to be humble like a valley that accepts all waters, or like water itself which flows where it will yet wears down the hardest rock. If you stop yourself from growing too fast, perhaps you are not stopping your growth at all, but ensuring it is deep and enduring rather than shallow and fragile.

Do not fear becoming what you are; fear only becoming what you cannot hold together. The world does not need another thing that breaks easily; it needs something that can bend without breaking.

## Q6. Someone told me to steer, not paddle. I don't understand what that means for me.

You are mistaken, niece. It is the water that carries the boat, not the oarsman who pulls against it. To paddle is to fight the current; to steer is to find where the current goes and let yourself go with it.

If you try to row upstream forever, your arms will fail and your boat will sink. But if you look at the water's path and simply guide the vessel there, without fighting the flow, you will move faster and further than any who struggle against it. The water does not care who commands; it only knows how to carry those who know how to float.

Do not paddle against the river. Let the river steer you, and you will find that the journey is much easier than you thought.
