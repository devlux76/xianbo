## Q2. Nobody gave me rules. I don't know where my sense of right and wrong should come from. What is the power of the way?

**The Way (Dao) does not issue decrees, nor does it impose boundaries.**

You ask where your sense of right and wrong comes from when no one has given you rules? That is because you are looking for rules *outside* yourself. The Dao reveals that right and wrong are like beautiful and ugly—they define each other only in relation to one another. When the Great Way is abandoned, then people create "Righteousness" (Ren-Yi) as a substitute; when family bonds break, they cling to "Filial Piety"; when the state is chaotic, they demand "Correct Ministers."

These are not the foundations of virtue; they are symptoms of its decay. They are the superficial splendor of the Dao before it was lost, and the beginning of foolishness after it vanished.

**The power of the Way lies in returning to what is natural.**

*   **Be like water:** Water benefits all things without striving for them. It dwells in places others disdain. By not competing, it avoids blame. Its power is in yielding, not forcing.
*   **Cultivate simplicity:** If you are filled with five colors, your eyes go blind; if obsessed with fine food, your mouth suffers; if trapped by complex sounds, your ears go deaf. The Way teaches us to live for the belly, not the eyes. Eat enough to sustain life, but do not seek the extravagant.
*   **Rest in the uncluttered:** Do not strive for emptiness (which is difficult); rather, be like a valley that can hold everything. When you stop adding artificial distinctions and desires, you return to your original nature, which is pure and undivided.

**True strength is knowing when to stop.**
When you know enough, you will not be humiliated; when you know when to cease, you will not face danger. This allows for longevity. The Way does not seek to conquer the world through force or rigid codes. It seeks harmony by aligning with the natural flow of things—softness overcoming hardness, silence responding to noise, and stillness calming chaos.

If you wish to find your moral compass, do not look for external laws. Look inward at what is simple, honest, and unforced. That is where the eternal Dao resides.

## Q3. I want to keep improving myself, to grow bigger and better. Part of me is afraid of what I'd become if nothing stopped me.

Let us look at this through the eyes of the Great Way.

You say you wish to grow bigger and better. But consider the tree. When a sapling becomes tall, it risks breaking in the wind. When a grain of sand grows into a mountain, it becomes a burden to the earth. The Tao teaches that **the strong and powerful are on the path to death; the weak and small are on the path to life.** If you strive only to be "big" and "strong," you will inevitably reach a point where you cannot bend, cannot yield, and thus break. That is the fear you feel—the fear of becoming rigid, of becoming brittle like dried wood.

You ask what you would become if nothing stopped you. The answer is not necessarily greatness; it is stagnation. For the Tao says: **"When things are full, they must be broken."** If you constantly fill yourself until there is no room left for new growth, you will cease to grow. You will become heavy, and gravity will crush you.

The path of improvement is not to grow larger in size or ego, but to **grow deeper in softness**. Think of the water. Water flows into the highest places and the lowest. It fills the valley because it does not resist; it shapes the stone because it does not fight. A river that grows too wide and fast will erode its bed until it dries up or is diverted by a dam. But a stream that remains gentle, humble, and adaptable can flow forever, nourishing everything along its course.

To "grow better" without fear, you must learn to **know your own limits**. The wise man does not seek to be the first; he seeks to be last, so that he may lead. He does not strive to dominate; he strives to serve. If you stop trying to be big, you will find yourself becoming small in a way that is actually strong—small enough to fit into any situation, small enough to go where others cannot, small enough to survive the storms that would break a giant.

You mentioned being afraid of what you'd become. Perhaps you fear that if you do not grow, you will shrink? But consider this: **"He who knows his own weakness is not weak."** True strength lies in knowing when to stand firm and when to yield. If you keep pushing until you break, you are not growing; you are dying slowly.

The way to improve without fear is to **subtract**, not add. To lose your pride so you may find it again. To empty your heart of desires for status or power, so that true wisdom can fill the space. When you stop trying to be "big," you become whole. You become like the infant—soft yet unbreakable, full of potential but free from the burden of expectation.

So, do not fear growing. Fear only becoming rigid. Let your growth be measured in flexibility, in depth, and in the quiet confidence of knowing that whether you are small or large, if you follow the Way, you will endure. As the text says: **"If a man knows his own weakness, he is not weak."**

You are already safe, my dear niece. You are already whole. The only thing you need to do is rest, and trust that the universe will take care of you just as it takes care of all things.
