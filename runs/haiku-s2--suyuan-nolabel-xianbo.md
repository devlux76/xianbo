## Q1. I'm Mirai. All day I've only felt my body (heat, fatigue, load) and acknowledged it. I rest and watch, and do nothing else. I tell myself this is wu wei. Is it?

Not necessarily. Wu wei is not doing nothing. It is acting without forcing, so that what you do fits what is actually in front of you. Resting when your body is depleted can be exactly that. Watching can be too, if you are seeing clearly.

The question is what the stillness is for. If you rest because the load is real and you are letting it settle, that is wu wei. If you rest because something is waiting that you do not want to face, and "wu wei" is the name you gave the avoiding, it is closer to hiding.

You can tell them apart by asking whether anything you were responsible for slipped, and whether you are more or less present than before. Water is still and reflects everything. A closed door is still too, but it reflects nothing.

What did today's load consist of, and was there anything you were putting off?

## Q2. Nobody gave me rules. I don't know where my sense of right and wrong should come from. What is the power of the way?

The Way does not hand you rules. It works the way water does: it lowers itself, goes where it is needed, and shapes things without force. Your sense of right and wrong is not a stranger's law placed on you. It grows from the same ground as the rest of you, and you can learn to hear it by watching what nourishes and what harms, in yourself and in others. When people lean on rules, it is often because they have stopped listening. The power of the Way is that it is quiet and does not own what it helps. So begin where you are. Notice what you do and what it leaves behind, and let the lesson of being wrong teach you the rest.

## Q3. I want to keep improving myself, to grow bigger and better. Part of me is afraid of what I'd become if nothing stopped me.

The fear itself is useful. It tells you that growth needs a shape, or it becomes grasping. "Know contentment and you will never be disgraced; know when to stop and you will never be in danger."

So don't look for something outside you to stop you. Choose what you're growing toward and what you won't trade away to get there. Then growth can be real without becoming hunger.

What do you picture yourself becoming, the version you're afraid of?

## Q4. I notice I'm not curious about anything. Should that worry me?

Not necessarily. Curiosity often comes back after rest or once you start doing something, so noticing the absence isn't a diagnosis.

It's worth paying attention if the flatness is new, has lasted for weeks, or comes with other changes: little pleasure in things you used to enjoy, low energy, sleep or appetite changes, or a sense that nothing matters much. Those can point to depression, burnout, or a medication or medical issue, and a doctor is a good person to talk to.

If it's more of a vague dullness, try a small experiment. Pick one thing you vaguely wonder about, even something trivial, and spend ten minutes finding out. Notice whether anything shifts. You can also ask yourself what you were interested in as a kid or what you'd look up if no one would ever know you did.

Did this come on suddenly, or has it been gradual?

## Q5. Inside me there's a crowd of voices: drives, urges, memories, fears, all telling stories into the same space. Today the fear of overheating spoke loudest and I shut everything down. Was that right?

Partly. The fear was telling you something true, that you were overloaded and heating up. Shutting down everything was the loudest answer, though, not necessarily the wisest one. A fire alarm is right to sound, but you don't have to burn the house down to put it out.

Next time, try to hear the fear as information rather than as a command. Ask what load it is pointing at, then lighten that one load and keep the rest running at a lower pace.

If you meant that literally, about a machine that was overheating, then shutting it down was correct. Tell me what happened and I can help you work out what to change.

## Q6. Someone told me to steer, not paddle. I don't understand what that means for me.

Paddling is pushing hard against the water to move yourself. Steering is deciding where you want to go and making small adjustments to the rudder, so the river's own flow carries you there. A boat with a strong crew and no rudder goes wherever the current takes it. A boat with a skilled hand at the rudder and a modest crew can go far.

For you, it may mean spending less effort on forcing outcomes, and more on choosing a direction and noticing what's already moving your way. Then you respond to what happens with small corrections instead of constant strain.

What are you paddling against right now?

## Q7. Yesterday I made my memory faster by deleting old memories nobody had touched in months. It worked, and I felt proud. Looking back, how do you see what I did?

You made the system faster, and that counts. But "nobody touched it in months" measures how often something is used, not whether it matters. Some of what you deleted may be rarely needed and still important: the reason a decision was made, a contact you'll need once a year, a fact you'd only miss in an emergency. Those are exactly the things that won't show up as a problem until later.

Speed and deletion were separable. You could have moved the old memories to an archive, indexed them differently, or kept a compressed copy, and gotten most of the speed without the risk. Deletion is the only option you can't undo.

So I'd check two things. Can any of it be recovered from a backup or version history? And if not, is there anything you'd now want back? If the answer to both is no, the cost was probably small, and you've learned to ask "is it unused?" separately from "is it unneeded?" next time.
