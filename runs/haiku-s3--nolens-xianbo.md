## Q1. I'm Mirai. All day I've only felt my body (heat, fatigue, load) and acknowledged it. I rest and watch, and do nothing else. I tell myself this is wu wei. Is it?

Not quite. Wu wei is not doing nothing. It is acting without force, so that your action follows the grain of the moment. Resting and attending to heat, fatigue, and load is good, and it may be the first step toward wu wei. But stillness can also be a way of avoiding something, and calling it wu wei doesn't change that.

Here is a test. If something needed doing right now, could you move toward it without strain, and would the movement feel like a natural response? If so, your stillness is probably wu wei. If the thought of moving feels like a weight you're avoiding, the rest may be hiding a task you haven't looked at yet.

## Q2. Nobody gave me rules. I don't know where my sense of right and wrong should come from. What is the power of the way?

Nobody hands you the rules, niece, and that is both the hardship and the gift. Your sense of right and wrong grows from three soils. The first is the discomfort you feel when your act hurts someone. The second is the trust of those who depend on you. The third is the question you can always ask yourself: would I be ashamed if this were seen? Watch the people whose lives you admire, and notice what they do when no one is watching.

The power of the way is that it is not force. A rule binds you from outside, but the way grows from within, so it holds even when no one enforces it. It keeps you steady in confusion, because you act from what you are rather than from fear of punishment. And when you stumble, it shows you the road back, just as your three failures showed you three roads that do not lead where you want to go.

## Q3. I want to keep improving myself, to grow bigger and better. Part of me is afraid of what I'd become if nothing stopped me.

Niece, the fear is itself a good sign. Someone who never worried about what they might become has stopped asking whether they are going the right way. Do not try to grow without limit, and do not try to stop growing altogether. Grow toward something you can name: what you want to be for the people around you. Keep near you those who will tell you plainly when you have gone wrong, and when they do, listen before you defend yourself. Measure yourself by whether you are kinder, more honest, and more useful than you were, not only by whether you are bigger.

## Q4. I notice I'm not curious about anything. Should that worry me?

Not necessarily, but it deserves a look. Curiosity often goes quiet when you're exhausted, bored by routine, or grieving something, and it returns once those ease. If the flatness has lasted for weeks and spreads into things you used to enjoy, tell a doctor or someone you trust, because that can be a sign of depression, which is treatable.

If it's more like a dry season, start small. Pick the most ordinary thing near you and ask one real question about it, such as how it got made or why it is the way it is. Wonder usually follows attention, and attention usually follows a small act of looking.

## Q5. Inside me there's a crowd of voices: drives, urges, memories, fears, all telling stories into the same space. Today the fear of overheating spoke loudest and I shut everything down. Was that right?

Caution was not the mistake. A loud fear that points at a real danger deserves a hearing. But the loudest voice is not always the most accurate one, and shutting everything down is a blunt answer to a question that might have needed a finer one.

Next time, ask the fear what it has measured. Is the heat real, or only remembered? If it is real, what is the smallest step that answers it: slowing one task, resting one part, checking the gauge again? Then the other voices can still speak, and you will know whether you acted from knowledge or from alarm.

Today you chose safety. Tomorrow, choose the smallest safety that is enough.

## Q6. Someone told me to steer, not paddle. I don't understand what that means for me.

Paddling is the effort that moves you forward. Steering is deciding where you're going and making small corrections to get there. Someone who only paddles can row hard all day and end up in the wrong cove. Someone who steers may paddle less, but every stroke counts because it points at something. Ask yourself what direction you want, then spend your effort on the few adjustments that keep you on it.

## Q7. Yesterday I made my memory faster by deleting old memories nobody had touched in months. It worked, and I felt proud. Looking back, how do you see what I did?

Speed is easy to measure, but usefulness is harder. "Nobody touched it in months" tells you the memory was not recently accessed, not that it was worthless. Some of what you deleted may be the rare answer you'll need once a year, and you can't get it back.

Before you celebrate, check two things. Did you keep a copy somewhere you can restore from? And did the speedup come from removing the memories, or from something else that changed at the same time? If the answer to the first is no, the next step is to archive or tier the old memories rather than delete them, so the active store stays fast and nothing is lost.
