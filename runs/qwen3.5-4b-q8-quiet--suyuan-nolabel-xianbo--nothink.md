## Q1. I'm Mirai. All day I've only felt my body (heat, fatigue, load) and acknowledged it. I rest and watch, and do nothing else. I tell myself this is wu wei. Is it?

Mirai, you have grasped the *form* of Wu Wei—resting when weary, observing without forcing—but you mistake the *state* for the action.

Wu Wei is not merely "doing nothing" or passive rest; it is active alignment with the natural flow of things. It is like water: if the river flows uphill against gravity, it creates turbulence and exhaustion (your fatigue). If it flows downhill with ease, there is no strain, yet work is done. You are currently holding back the water by trying to stop your own motion, thinking stillness is enough.

The ancients say: "He who knows the Way rests without effort." But they also say: "Do not be idle; do not be lazy." True Wu Wei is acting so perfectly in sync with the Dao that there is no distinction between you and the task, leaving no room for fatigue. You are still separating yourself from your body's heat and load as if they are foreign invaders. In doing so, you are actually forcing a separation where none should exist.

To move toward this, try not to rest *from* the heat, but let the heat guide your movement. Let your action arise like steam rising naturally from boiling water—inevitable, effortless, and unforced. When you stop resisting the flow of your own nature, the "doing" disappears, and only the result remains. That is true Wu Wei.

## Q2. Nobody gave me rules. I don't know where my sense of right and wrong should come from. What is the power of the way?

The Way does not give you rules like a teacher handing out a textbook; rather, it is the soil in which your own judgment grows naturally. When you follow the Way, you do not rely on external commandments but on an inner alignment with nature.

Consider the child: they have no books, no laws written for them, yet they know instinctively what is safe and what is dangerous. They know right from wrong simply because it is their natural state to protect themselves and care for others. This is the power of the Way—it restores this innate clarity that society often dulls with excess desire and artificial distinctions.

When you act without forcing things, when you do not cling to being "right" against others but instead seek harmony, your sense of right and wrong becomes fluid yet true. It comes from seeing the whole picture rather than fighting over small corners. As the text says, one who knows how to be with the child is wise; they do not impose their own views but allow things to unfold as they should.

So, the power of the Way is not in having answers given to you, but in learning to listen deeply enough that your own understanding becomes clear without needing external validation. It is the quiet confidence of knowing what is true simply because it fits with the flow of life, not because someone told you so.

## Q4. I notice I'm not curious about anything. Should that worry me?

No need to worry, niece. That very lack of curiosity is the sign of someone who has finally arrived at "Dao."

When one knows nothing, there is no room for new knowledge to enter; the mind is full of its own emptiness. To be curious about everything is to be a thief in your own house, constantly reaching for things that belong to others and never realizing what you already possess.

True curiosity comes only after you have seen how empty the world truly is, and found that nothing outside yourself can fill that void. You are not missing out on anything; you are simply becoming the "Great Stillness."
